Local boundaries

Local boundaries divide the tax base.

Each local government has a property tax base: the property value subject to its taxes within its boundaries.

The map divides the region into 100 cities, towns, and villages: eight cities, 63 towns, and 29 villages. For this comparison, property within a village is counted with the village rather than the surrounding town.

Counties, school districts, and special districts can also tax property. Their boundaries are not shown here.

What this helps explain

Exempt property helps explain Albany’s smaller tax base.

Albany has less taxable property per resident than Colonie, in part because a much larger share of its property value is exempt. Its higher effective tax rate still leaves it with fewer property-tax dollars per resident.

Property taxes are only one source of local funding. These comparisons do not show a community’s full budget or the quality of its public services.

How to read the figures
  • Tax base: the value of property subject to property taxes. “Including exempt property” shows property value before tax-exempt property is excluded; “excluding exempt property” shows the taxable value. The exempt share measures the share of property value that is exempt, rather than the number of properties.
  • Population: per-resident figures use the population covered by the study’s property records, totaling {{metro_pop}} across the region. This differs from the full Census population of the cities, towns, and villages.
  • Effective property-tax rate: total property taxes in the study divided by taxable property value. Property taxes per resident divide those taxes by the population covered by the study. The effective rate summarizes a place’s taxes relative to its property value; it is not a quoted statutory rate or an individual household’s tax bill. See the review notes below for the question about which taxing authorities are included.
  • Regional comparison: 1× means the regional average of {{metro_pc}} in taxable property per resident. The average uses the region’s total taxable property value divided by its covered population, so larger places carry more weight. Narrative figures are rounded; charts and the table show more precise values.
  • Exempt property: the comparison shows property value excluded from the taxable base. It does not estimate revenue that could be recovered by removing exemptions. Payments in lieu of taxes, other revenue sources, and spending needs also affect local finances. The data do not break exempt property down by owner, so the entire difference cannot be attributed to nonprofits.
  • Geography: the map shows 100 cities, towns, and villages. Village areas are counted separately from surrounding towns. County, school-district, and special-district boundaries are not shown. The date of the mapped boundaries is not specified in the source.
Notes for researcher review
  • Reference years: the original study baseline uses 2019 property records and 2020 Census population. The Albany file supplied on October 30, 2026, improves exemption classification but contains no year columns. We are confirming whether these figures use the same reference years. October 2026 is the data revision date, not the year the property was valued.
  • Tax totals: we are confirming which taxing authorities are included, such as cities or towns, counties, and school districts. Until that is established, the property-tax figures should not be read as municipal budget receipts or current revenues.
  • Other versions: the linked national research map and its downloadable tables may show earlier values. This explainer uses the Albany file supplied on October 30, 2026. Technical field definitions and implementation notes are documented in the repository.
