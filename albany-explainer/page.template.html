Local boundaries

Property falls on different sides of a line.

Each local government has a property tax base: the property value subject to its taxes within its boundaries.

This map separates 100 local governments: eight cities, 63 towns, and 29 villages. In the study, property is assigned to the smallest general-purpose local government that can tax it. Village territory is separated from the surrounding town.

Counties, school districts, and special districts can also tax property. Their boundaries are not shown here.

What this helps explain

Boundaries and exemptions shape what a place can tax.

A smaller tax base means that the same rate produces fewer property-tax dollars per resident. Albany’s much larger exempt share further separates the property value within its borders from the value available to tax.

These figures describe the study’s property values and tax measures. They do not establish current municipal revenues, spending, or service quality. Transfers, other revenue sources, payments in lieu of taxes, and spending needs also matter.

The value excluded as exempt is not an estimate of revenue that could be recovered by removing exemptions.

How the figures are calculated, and what remains to confirm
  • Tax base: pc_value_tot, the value per resident after the updated exemption exclusions. Before exclusions: pc_value_raw. The exempt share is 1 − value_tot / value_raw, a share of property value, not a count of properties.
  • Population: all per-capita measures use clpop, the study-covered population. The regional total is {{metro_pop}}. This differs from full Census jurisdiction totals in cenpop.
  • Effective property-tax rate: taxrt_tot = tax_tot / value_tot. Property taxes per resident are pc_tax_tot = tax_tot / clpop. These are aggregate study measures, not a quoted statutory rate or an individual household’s tax bill. The scope of taxing authorities and relationship to municipal budget receipts remain to be confirmed with the researcher.
  • Regional comparison: fiscal-capacity ratio equals pc_value_tot divided by the regional taxable value per covered resident, {{metro_pc}}. The regional average is calculated from summed values and population, not a simple average of local figures.
  • Years: the original study baseline uses 2019 property records and 2020 Census population. The revised extract improves exemption classification; it contains no year columns. Confirmation that all updated measures retain those reference years is pending. “Revised October 2026” identifies the data version, not the property year.
  • Exemptions: the difference includes properties excluded by the revised classification. This extract does not break exemptions down by ownership or estimate tax losses net of payments in lieu of taxes. It cannot attribute the entire gap to nonprofits.
  • Geography: all 100 records join to the original city, town, and village geometries using state, jurisdiction, and CBSA identifiers. Village areas remain separate from surrounding towns. The source layer does not specify a boundary vintage.
  • Versions: the linked national research map and original downloadable tables may carry earlier values. This explainer consistently uses the revised Albany extract. Residential shares and the earlier fragmentation quotient are omitted because updated equivalents were not supplied.
